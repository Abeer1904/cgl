Climate & Resilience

Playable
climate

Climate change is no longer a distant or abstract crisis. Communities, local governments, and institutions are already experiencing its effects — through heatwaves, floods, droughts, water stress, changing agrarian patterns, and livelihood uncertainty.

The challenge is not only to communicate climate change. The challenge is to help people visualise its consequences, understand its local causes, identify pathways for action, and make decisions today that can build resilience five or ten years from now.

The climate governance problem

Three gaps that
block climate action.

Communities and local governments are being asked to respond to climate change without always having the language, tools, or institutional spaces to do so. Many people experience climate change through heat, failed crops, water scarcity, floods, and livelihood stress — but these experiences are often not connected to larger systems of land use, public finance, local planning, and ecological change.

01
The Vocabulary Gap

Communities need accessible ways to understand climate concepts in relation to their lived reality — heat stress, groundwater depletion, biodiversity loss, carrying capacity, mitigation, adaptation, and resilience. Without shared language, climate conversations cannot begin.

02
The Imagination Gap

People need to visualise how climate change can alter their village, city, ecosystem, water systems, livelihoods, and future choices. Many know that risk is increasing, but still struggle to imagine how today's decisions can shape tomorrow's vulnerability.

03
The Planning Gap

Elected representatives, administrators, businesses, and communities need spaces where they can sit together, test interventions, understand trade-offs, and identify actions that can be seeded now. Most governance formats do not provide this space.

Civic Games Lab responds to these three gaps through a layered climate methodology. At its heart is a shift from knowledge brokerage to innovation brokerage — not only helping people know more about climate change, but helping them identify, adapt, test, and seed solutions in their own regions.

Our approach

Five stages from
vocabulary to capability.

Our climate methodology moves participants through five linked stages — each one building on the last, each one with its own set of tools designed for a specific kind of climate learning.

Stage 02

01

Our climate methodology

Build Climate
Vocabulary

Climate action begins with language. Communities need simple, localised ways to understand concepts such as heat stress, groundwater depletion, biodiversity loss, forest fires, hydrological systems, wetlands, carrying capacity, land degradation, mitigation, adaptation, and resilience.

These tools are deliberately simple. Their purpose is conversation, identification, repetition, and recall. Players learn by looking, finding, naming, and discussing — and they leave with vocabulary they can use in their own environment.

Stage 01
Climate Crusaders

Players engage with large illustrated landscapes inspired by visual discovery. They search for clues, solve word puzzles, and identify climate phenomena hidden in everyday settings — building vocabulary through recognition and naming.
Stage 01
Climate Quest

Players match images, concepts, causes, and outcomes — understanding how specific actions lead to specific environmental consequences. Vocabulary becomes analytical capacity through cause-and-effect card play.
Water literacy
Amrit Jal

Players identify water problems and best practices within an illustrated village landscape — recognising groundwater depletion, wasteful behaviour, damaged commons, and missing institutional coordination as part of their own environment.

02

Localise
Climate Risk

Once participants have the vocabulary, they must connect it to their own surroundings. Climate change cannot remain a global idea. It must be localised to the drying well, the failed crop, the hotter school day, the polluted road, the degraded commons, the flood-prone settlement, or the changing work available to young people.

This stage helps participants move from "climate change is happening" to "climate change is changing our place."

Water governance
Integrated Water Management

The game places water stress inside a recognisable village landscape. Players identify the drying well, the depleted groundwater, the damaged commons, and the missing institutional coordination as part of their own lived environment — before entering the simulation.

Narrative entry
Amrit-Jal Nagar: Paani ki Kahani

Through the story of Arjun, his father Rajan, and Dada Bholanath, the groundwater crisis becomes human before it becomes technical. The drying well, the worried Pradhan, and the government engineer become entry points into a wider system of water governance — emotion before analysis.

03

Experience
Climate Futures

Climate change is difficult to act on because many of its consequences unfold unevenly and over time. Communities may know that risk is increasing, but still struggle to imagine how today's choices can shape tomorrow's vulnerability.

This is where live-action role-playing, scenarios, story-based exercises, and physical simulations become essential. Climate preparedness is not only cognitive. It is social, emotional, embodied, and political — and only certain formats can reach those registers.

LARP format
Flood & Drought Scenarios

Participants physically move through a flood or drought scenario — taking decisions, embodying roles, facing constraints, and experiencing consequences through narrative and interaction. When decisions are delayed and warnings ignored, participants feel the cost before they understand it analytically.

Scenario format
Agrarian Transition Scenarios

Heatwave, water stress, and changing agrarian pattern scenarios help participants experience what happens when resources are unevenly distributed and coordination fails. The scenario creates a bridge between awareness and anticipatory practice.

Microdramas
Climate Microdramas

Short story-based exercises that make climate futures visible through character and consequence. Microdramas reach registers that data and reports cannot — building emotional preparedness alongside analytical understanding.

04

Simulate Systems
& Trade-offs

After vocabulary and visualisation, participants need to test decisions inside systems. Climate change is not only an environmental issue — it is a planning issue, a livelihood issue, a land-use issue, a public finance issue, and a governance issue.

This is where system-first simulations become central. Participants learn that adaptation requires foresight, timing, resources, and strategy — and that every ecosystem and every city has carrying capacity that development choices can either regenerate or degrade.

Ecological simulation
Biomes

Players manage ecosystems while balancing land use, population needs, employment, ecological health, and climate risk. Biome cards, land-use cards, climate-change cards, and eco-tokens show that every ecosystem has carrying capacity — and that development choices can regenerate or degrade the land.

Stage 04
Fix It!

Urban air pollution as a playable city system. Land use, infrastructure, stakeholder agendas, wind direction, regulation, and urban expansion interact — showing that air pollution is not a single-issue problem but a consequence of connected planning choices.
Water governance
Integrated Water Management

Communities simulate water governance across seasons. Players allocate limited Gram Panchayat funds across competing water needs — learning water budgeting, scheme literacy, seasonal planning, and the trade-offs between immediate demands and long-term water security.

05

Planning &
Innovation Brokerage

The final stage is not just learning. It is action. Our climate framework is built to help institutions, local governments, elected representatives, businesses, and communities identify solutions, adapt them to local conditions, and seed interventions.

Participants engage with innovations, best practices, everyday actions, schemes, financing mechanisms, infrastructure options, and planning pathways. They test what may work, what may fail, what requires coordination, and what needs to be sequenced — moving from information-sharing to co-creation.

Two platforms extend this stage into institutional and district-scale planning: the Just Transition Lab and the Climate Smart Village framework.

Innovation platforms at Stage 05

Platform
JTL
Just Transition Lab

The Just Transition Lab is CGL's institutional planning platform for organisations, governments, and communities navigating energy, livelihood, and land-use transitions. It combines simulation, scenario design, and co-creation workshops to help institutions test transition pathways before committing to them.

JTL is where the climate methodology meets institutional decision-making — connecting the vocabulary and systems understanding built in stages 1–4 to the actual planning processes of state governments, businesses, and multilateral partners.

From knowledge
brokerage to
innovation brokerage.

We do not only want people to know more about climate change. We want them to identify, adapt, test, and seed solutions in their own regions.

This is the core of innovation brokerage: creating spaces where communities and institutions can learn from the wider universe of climate actions, test them in relation to their own realities, and identify what can be implemented locally — with the schemes, resources, and institutional support that already exist.

The objective is not only climate communication. The objective is climate capability.

What our climate tools enable

Climate capability
across scales.

Shared vocabulary

A common language for climate change, water, ecosystems, risk, and resilience — accessible to participants with no prior climate education.

Local recognition

Communities see climate change not as a global abstraction but as the drying well, the changing crop calendar, the hotter midday — their own environment, made legible.

Future imagination

Participants visualise possible futures shaped by heatwaves, floods, droughts, air pollution, groundwater depletion, and changing agrarian patterns — before those futures arrive.

Systems thinking

Climate change understood as a planning, livelihood, land-use, and governance issue — not only an environmental one. The connections that produce risk become visible.

Strategy testing

Adaptation and mitigation strategies tested before implementation — with the consequences of wrong choices visible, discussable, and reversible inside the simulation.

Scheme connection

Communities connect local problems to Amrit Sarovar, Atal Bhujal, PMKSY, and other schemes — turning climate awareness into a practical planning resource.

Anticipatory planning

Institutions develop the habit of planning for climate scenarios before they materialise — building adaptive capacity alongside responsive capacity.

Co-creation spaces

Collaborative environments for deliberation, knowledge sharing, and innovation brokerage — where communities and institutions design solutions together, not for each other.
