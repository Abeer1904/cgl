Digital & Society

The Internet
You Live In.

Technology is evolving faster than communities can use, understand, or govern it. We build games that close that gap — making digital systems legible, placing rights in the hands of communities, and rehearsing the governance decisions that shape whose internet this actually is.

From digital rights and online safety to AI governance and internet fragmentation — six games, one fictional city, and a methodology built from eight years of field research.

The digital problem

Three gaps technology
creates — and the field
does not fill.

The rate of technological change far outstrips adoption, understanding, and governance. Communities are left navigating systems they cannot see, rights they do not know they hold, and ecosystems they have no power to shape.

01
The Legibility Gap

Emergent technologies — AI, algorithmic platforms, data systems — evolve faster than communities can use, understand, or adopt them. Making technology legible is not optional. It is the precondition for everything else.

02
The Agency Gap

Private platforms providing public goods. Misinformation and disinformation at scale. Data rights, collective bargaining, and ethics frameworks that communities cannot access. The question is not only what technology does — but what it does to your rights.

03
The Ecosystem Gap

How do you build a resilient ecosystem for AI or other technological advancements? How do you straddle internal and external uncertainty — sovereignty, legitimacy, capacity, sustainability — without being captured by the fastest mover?

01

Stage 01 — Demystification

Make Technology
Legible.

The first challenge is comprehension. Technology that cannot be understood cannot be governed, used safely, or resisted effectively. Before players can assert rights, they need to see the system — how information travels, how misinformation spreads, what a digital right actually is, and why the rules of the internet are not neutral.

This stage builds a shared vocabulary: for fake news and how it circulates, for what digital rights protect and why they exist, for how laws respond to speech and how those responses can be weaponised. Games at this stage make visible what is usually invisible.

Stage 01 in: Farzi
Farzi

A fake news quiz show and card game. Divided by theme — players decide whether they would forward it or not. The core mechanic is identification: what does misinformation look like, and how does it travel?
Stage 01 in: Digital Naagrik
Digital Naagrik

A ludo-style board game in Antarjaal Nagar. Players collect 7 Rights Tokens while navigating real online threats — making rights legible as practical equipment rather than legal theory.
Stage 01 in: It's in the Water
It's in the Water

A crisis unfolds in Antarjaal Nagar. Players navigate defamation, freedom of expression, and how laws around speech can be modified — making legal frameworks legible through lived narrative.
02

Stage 02 — Online Safety

Navigate the
Threat Landscape.

Knowing that threats exist is different from knowing what they feel like. This stage places players inside threat scenarios — as the person being catfished, the activist being doxxed, the community navigating a misinformation crisis. The learning is visceral before it is analytical.

Players find the red flags in real-time chat logs. They jump through a phone like a side-scroller, collecting security tips while avoiding trolls. They assemble clues about a crisis. The point is not abstract risk — it is the specific, recognisable texture of how digital harm actually arrives.

Stage 02 in: Story of Kartik
Story of Kartik

A gay student is catfished by classmates using a fake profile. Players read his chats, identify the red flags, protect his profile, spot the fake image, and find care. Online harm made visible and navigable.
Stage 02 in: Kritika
Kritika

A young girl-activist is being threatened with doxxing. Players enter her phone like Mario — jumping to avoid trolls, collecting security tips to protect her profile. Digital self-defence as play.
Stage 02 in: Farzi
Farzi

Fake news as social threat. Players encounter misinformation the way it actually arrives — through cards that look credible, feel shareable, and carry a sting only visible when you know what to look for.

03

Stage 03 — Rights & Agency

Centre Rights
and Agency.

The internet is not neutral. It is the outcome of decisions — by platforms, regulators, coalitions, and citizens — each with a vision and a strategy. This stage shifts players from vulnerability to power: digital rights as practical survival tools, platform accountability as a contested space, and collective bargaining as the mechanism through which communities can shape the systems they live inside.

Players collect rights tokens as protective equipment. They form coalitions to resist internet fragmentation. They navigate the gap between what platforms owe users and what users can actually demand. The learning is that rights are not given — they are exercised.

Stage 03 in: Digital Naagrik
Digital Naagrik

7 Rights Tokens as protective equipment — earned by navigating the same threats real users face. Unverified information has collective costs; platform accountability (or its absence) affects everyone on the board.
Stage 03 in: Splinternet
Splinternet

Players hold secret agendas and vote on real-world internet policy statements. Coalition-forming, token-spending, and power cards reveal how regulatory capture works — and that there is no single villain driving fragmentation.
Stage 03 in: It's in the Water
It's in the Water

Freedom of expression as a right that can be contested, modified, and weaponised. Players navigate defamation law in a crisis — and discover that legal frameworks are not neutral instruments.
04

Stage 04 — Ecosystem Governance

Build and Test
Ecosystems.

The hardest question in digital governance is not how to use technology — it is how to build the conditions under which technology can be developed, governed, and contested at a national or institutional scale. Speed, sovereignty, legitimacy, and sustainability pull in opposite directions. Foreign investment and indigenous capacity are a genuine trade-off. The countries setting the rules of AI are not doing so neutrally.

Games at this stage are for policymakers, institutional leaders, and communities that need to rehearse ecosystem-level decisions before they harden into policy, procurement, or practice.

Stage 04 in: Splinternet
Splinternet

Internet fragmentation as a governance ecosystem — 3–7 players, secret agendas, competing interests. Fragmentation emerges from the system, not from any single villain. The internet you experience is an outcome of this game.
Simulation
AI Race

Solo or multi-table role-play as a National AI Advisor. Five decision arenas — compute, indigenous model, data governance, talent, geopolitics. Four scores: Sovereignty, Legitimacy, Capacity, Sustainability. You don't choose to be unethical in the AI race — you run out of time to be ethical.

Flagship Universe
Antarjaal
Nagar.
The Fictional Internet City

Antarjaal Nagar is the shared fictional universe across four of our digital games — a city that lives on the internet, where rights are real, threats are structural, and the laws that govern speech are contested daily. It gives players a consistent world to return to across different games and different stages of learning.

The city functions as a teaching platform: each game enters Antarjaal Nagar from a different angle — a water crisis, a board game, a girl-activist's phone, a young man's chat logs. The terrain changes. The rights, laws, and power structures of the city remain consistent.

This continuity is deliberate. It allows facilitators to sequence games across a programme, build on prior learning, and return to the same city with increasing complexity. Antarjaal Nagar is not a metaphor. It is infrastructure.

The threat may change — fake news, catfishing, AI sovereignty, internet fragmentation. The methodology remains stable: make the system visible, make the choices playable, make the consequences discussable.

From digital
user to digital
citizen.

We do not only want people to know more about technology. We want them to see themselves as rights-holders inside digital systems — with the vocabulary, tools, and coalitions to act on that position.

This is the core of our digital methodology: moving communities from passive consumption to active citizenship. Not just what the internet does to you — but what you can do inside it, and who you need to do it with.

The goal is not digital literacy. The goal is digital agency — and the institutional capacity to govern the systems that shape it.

What our digital tools enable

Digital capability
across scales.

Shared vocabulary

A common language for how digital systems work — algorithms, platforms, rights, misinformation — accessible to participants with no prior technical background.

Threat recognition

Players learn to spot catfishing, doxxing, fake news, and online harassment before those threats arrive in real life — through scenarios that feel real because they are built from real cases.

Rights as tools

Digital rights become practical survival equipment — not legal abstractions. Players leave knowing what specific rights protect, how to invoke them, and why platform accountability matters.

Coalition thinking

Internet governance is not individual. Players learn how coalitions form and break, how bluffing and legitimacy interact in policy spaces, and that collective action shapes the systems they use daily.

Policy intuition

How regulatory capture works. How internet fragmentation emerges from competing interests. How laws around speech can be modified — and what it costs when they are.

Ecosystem literacy

For institutions and policymakers: the genuine trade-offs in building AI or digital governance ecosystems — sovereignty vs. speed, legitimacy vs. capacity, national interest vs. global interdependence.

Identity and safety

Specific digital safety literacy for marginalised communities — queer youth, activists, women in public life — whose threat landscape is distinct and whose rights require specific, practical protection.

Institutional capacity

Organisations leave with replicable facilitation tools, scenario sets, and programme designs they can deploy across communities — building digital citizenship at scale, not in isolation.
